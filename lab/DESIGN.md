# INLAvanilla design decisions

Decisions agreed after `IDEA.md` was written. Where the two disagree, this file wins.

## Settled

- **One `logpost`.** The user supplies `logpost(theta, ...)` returning log p(y, θ). There is no `loglik` / `logprior` split. The log marginal likelihood is valid only when `logpost` includes every normalising constant, and the docs must say so.
- **Pure R.** No Rcpp and no `src/`. Users bring compiled code by passing any R-callable function, e.g. `Rcpp::cppFunction()`, TMB's `obj$fn` / `obj$gr` (negate them: TMB minimises), RTMB, or BridgeStan.
- **No numDeriv, no sn.** Port INLAvaan's own finite differences (`fast_grad`, `fast_jacobian`, `fast_hessian`, now `fd_*` with per-coordinate steps) and skew-normal code (`fit_skew_normal`, `dsnorm`, `psnorm`, `qsnorm_fast`, `owen_t`). Imports are limited to base packages (stats, graphics, utils) plus cli.
- **v1 has no transform layer.** `logpost` is written on the unconstrained scale ϑ ∈ ℝᵐ. Including any Jacobian is the user's job: a prior on σ with ϑ = log σ needs `+ theta["log_sigma"]`. The docs must stress this, because forgetting it gives the wrong posterior, not merely a worse approximation. `summary()` reports on the ϑ scale. Natural-scale or derived quantities, such as σ, Σ, or continuous-time SEM drift matrices, come from `sampling(fit, fun = ...)`, and the user summarises the draws. `summary()` takes no `fun` argument for now. Copula draws never call `logpost`, so `nsamp` can be large at almost no cost. The reason for this choice: real constraints are often joint and model-specific (covariance matrices, stable drift matrices), and no generic transform layer can express them.
- **S3, not S7.** S4 and R6 are ruled out. The fit is a classed list built by one internal constructor, `new_inla_vanilla()`. `summary()` returns a `summary.inla_vanilla` object with its own `print()` method. S7 would add an Imports entry and an `@` workaround for R < 4.3, for features we don't use (validated properties, inheritance, multiple dispatch).
- **cli is a required Imports entry.** It is used for formatted messages, warnings and errors (`cli_abort()` etc.) and for progress bars and steps, as in INLAvaan. It may be dropped at a later optimisation stage, but not before. Add it to `DESCRIPTION` with the first code that uses it, to avoid an unused-Imports NOTE.
- **VB mean correction on by default.** It feeds the log marginal likelihood (+½ δᵀHδ, as in INLAvaan) and LOO/WAIC. It is also a diagnostic of the skew-normal fit, because skew-normal means should agree with ϑ̃ (Prop. 3.4). In INLAvaan the skew-normal scan is centred at the mode ϑ*, so the VB shift does not move the marginals themselves.
- **Stages 2 and 3 are independent switches** (agreed after v0.1.0): `vb = TRUE` and `marginals = c("skewnormal", "gaussian")`. They are not a ladder: the skew-normal scans are centred at the mode, so VB never moves them, and skew-normal without VB skips the VB stage's cost, at most (m + 1)(m + 2) + 1 gradient calls per sweep with a gradient, or (m + 1)(m + 2) log-posterior calls without one (see the VB expectation below). `vb = FALSE, marginals = "gaussian"` is the Laplace approximation alone; `marginals = "gaussian"` alone is the VB-centred Gaussian. The value may be abbreviated (`"skew"`, `"gauss"`), as with `match.arg()`. Gaussian marginals are stored as skew-normals with α = 0, so every method works unchanged, and `sampling()` then draws from the joint Gaussian exactly.
- **Three output levels, `verbose = FALSE` and `silent = FALSE` by default** (agreed after v0.1.0: `verbose` is a full diagnostic of the fit, and `silent` silences everything, for simulations). The default is the old `verbose = TRUE` output, one line per stage, and must stay as fast. Progress bars respect the user's `cli.progress_show_after` (0.5 s when unset); rendered documents keep them out through options in their setup chunk, as INLAvaan's README does, rather than through a terminal check in the package. `verbose = TRUE` adds grey detail lines under each stage, from what the fit has already computed (no extra calls): nlminb's message, iterations and evaluations and whether it was restarted, the whitened gradient and the Newton step at the mode, how the Hessian was taken, the largest correlation, the smallest eigenvalue of the correlation matrix and the Hessian condition number, the VB steps per iteration, Anderson and fallback use, a table of the marginal diagnostics (unscanned mass included) with the `summary()` flags marked, and the calls per stage. nlminb's own trace stays with `control = list(nlminb = list(trace = 1))`. `silent = TRUE` prints nothing and muffles every warning and message raised during the fit, including those of `logpost` and `grad`; errors still stop it. Both `TRUE` is an error. Whatever the level, each distinct warning is recorded as plain text in `fit$warnings`, and the fit stores `verbose` and `silent`, so later methods (`loo()` and `waic()`) can follow `fit$silent`.
- **Diagnostics match INLAvaan's** (owner's decision after v0.1.0: "where possible similar between both"). One function, `diagnostics_internal()` (`R/diagnostics.R`), computes INLAvaan's global and per-parameter diagnostics from the stored parts of the fit; the fit-time warning, the exported `diagnostics()` and, through `check_flags()` (`R/methods.R`), the flags of `print()` and `summary()` all read it, so they cannot disagree.
  - **Fit-time warning.** One `inlavanilla_diagnostics_warning`, headed as INLAvaan's ("Fit diagnostics flagged N potential issue(s):"), with INLAvaan's bullets, words and order: the mode shift, NMAD, the unscanned mass, the VB shift and the Hessian condition number, with two bullets of inlavanilla's own: the VB settling check right after the mode shift (with a hint, which differs for Gaussian marginals, centred on the VB mean), and the heavy tails after the unscanned mass. NMAD and unscanned mass list at most three parameters, worst first, with values to two decimals (INLAvaan's format, which can print a value just above a level as the level itself); tails, a yes/no check, lists at most three names, in parameter order; the mode and VB bullets name the worst parameter. Parameter names never enter cli template text: each bullet has its own variables, referred to by index, because names can hold braces and the templates are filled lazily (commit 9d1ced2). The footer points to `diagnostics()` and the vignette.
  - **The mode.** INLAvaan's `mode_shift_sigma` = |(Σg)ⱼ|/sⱼ decides the warning, above 0.1 posterior SDs, and the ok/! of the Laplace stage line. It is scale-free and does not depend on the order of the parameters. The old separate warning on the whitened gradient (above 0.01) is gone; its nlminb hint is an "i" line under the mode bullet. The whitened gradient `grad_mode` still decides the 1e-3 restart and is shown by `verbose = TRUE`. INLAvaan's bullet for a non-zero `nlminb()` return code is not ported, because without a gradient nlminb reports false convergence at accurate modes; `diagnostics()` reports `converged` as INLAvaan defines it, documented as unused.
  - **Hessian condition number.** INLAvaan's `hess_cond` and its 1e8 level, but scale-free: the eigenvalue ratio of the posterior correlation matrix, i.e. the Hessian in posterior-SD units. INLAvaan's raw definition fires on healthy fits whose posterior SDs differ widely (SDs 1e-5 and 1e3 give 1e16).
  - **VB.** `vb_shift` is the raw shift and `vb_shift_sigma` the shift in posterior SDs, as in INLAvaan; `kld` = `vb_shift_sigma²/2` per parameter, and the joint ½δᵀHδ is `kld_global`. With `vb = FALSE` the VB columns and `mean_gap` are NA, as INLAvaan leaves them. The VB fallback is only recorded, as in INLAvaan (no bullet, no flag, no "!" on the stage line): a forced fallback (`control$vb_maxit = 1`) on a healthy fit used to warn.
  - **`summary()`** flags every warned check at the warning's level (NMAD moved from 0.05 to 0.1, INLAvaan's only NMAD level), plus `ridge_bend` (0.25 nats) and `mean_gap` (0.1), inlavanilla's own, with "above" and a value that is not finite flagged. Its flagged lines list every parameter, worst first, with values (names only for tails; no truncation: on PolDem a top three would hide `logv_x2`, which MCMC shows is badly off). The table has VB shift, NMAD, Unscanned, Ridge bend and Mean gap, in the order of the status lines; the skew-normal shape `alpha` was dropped from it in October 2026 as informational, not a check (it stays in `diagnostics()`). Numbers are formatted as a tibble prints them (the owner's choice), to two significant figures, with flagged cells red and no other marker. One "✔" line gives the largest value of each skew-normal check that passes; there is a Hessian line for m > 1. Printed SDs are "σ" (" SD" in ASCII): every SD here is a posterior SD. Printed levels use ">" ("Ridge bend > 0.25", "warning > 0.1 SD"), and the predicted ridge-bend shortfall is ">10%": the flag is strict, and a ridge bend of 0.25 is already a 9.9% shortfall. There is no ASCII "≥", and none is needed. **Layout (October 2026, the owner's).** Output is at most 80 columns (`out_width()`), with soft wraps; the docs render at 76 because knitr's "#> " adds three and pkgdown's code box holds 79 at a 1024-pixel window (measured on the site). `summary()` goes fit → posterior marginals → diagnostics, with cli-style "── Title ──" headings. The accent colour is a vanilla-pod amber (`#B5772E`), replacing cyan, which the owner disliked; row names are plain. The tables of `print()` and `summary()` number their rows in grey, as a tibble does (the owner doubted it for `print()`, then chose consistency), and the posterior table lines up its decimal points, as the diagnostics table does. It keeps its precision per row, three significant figures of the posterior SD, rather than pillar's per number, which would print a mean of 102.35 with SD 0.05, and its whole interval, as "102.". Parameters on very different scales make the aligned columns wide (13 characters for 102.3500, 0.001200 and -12345), and the table then splits into blocks rather than going scientific. **Converged (October 2026, agreed with the owner).** "Converged" answers "did the computation reach its solution?", not "is the approximation good?", as a converged optimiser says nothing of model fit. Mode and Hessian are the whole solution only when the answer is the Laplace Gaussian; here the VB correction is a second iterative solve whose mean feeds the log marginal likelihood, `loo()`/`waic()` and the Gaussian marginals. So converged = the mode check and the VB check both pass: no further Newton step at the mode, nor fixed-point step at the VB mean, would move a parameter by more than 0.1 SDs. The VB step is free on the fixed-point path (below `vb_tol` by construction) and one sweep after the fallback, whose landing point was unchecked before. Left out of converged: the Hessian condition number (weak identification is a property of the posterior with a different remedy; lme4 lumps it into convergence warnings, a known source of confusion) and the skew-normal fits (a failed curve fit and an inadequate skew-normal both show as NMAD, with the same remedy). A non-positive-definite Hessian is an error, so every returned fit has one. The headline opens `print()` and `summary()` (the owner moved it up from below the table, for consistency with `summary()`'s fit → parameters → diagnostics), as lme4, glmmTMB and brms put fit statistics above the estimates; the iterations are `nlminb()`'s and the time the whole fit's. On the fixed-point path the VB step is the last step taken, which bounds the next; a `vb_tol` above 0.1 would make that bound useless, so the step is then taken afresh (a review found the false "did not settle" this caused). Without a gradient the fallback's objective (the rule mean of `logpost`) and its gradient (Stein's identity) disagree to the rule's error, so on strongly skewed posteriors `nlminb()` can stop 0.1–0.15 SDs short of Stein's root and the check reports it. **Decided (October 2026): no extra machinery for the no-gradient route.** The method's promise is made with a gradient; without one it should be cheap, good on the posteriors most people fit, and honest otherwise. Measured on one-parameter posteriors against exact VB (Gauss–Hermite): the fallback's objective aims at the rule's objective maximiser, which is exactly the with-gradient answer, and lands near it; polishing to Stein's root (2–4 sweeps) made the error worse (0.18 → 0.30 and 0.0001 → 0.12 SDs), and checking the fallback against its own target by finite differences costs m sweeps (65,000 calls at m = 39), over the VB budget. So the computation is unchanged for that route; an unsettled correction without a gradient gets a hint to supply `grad`, and the docs give the no-gradient accuracy honestly (median 0.015 SDs, up to about 0.1 on strongly skewed posteriors, about 0.03 with a gradient). **With a gradient (October 2026):** a 1D boundary posterior (a + 1 ~ Gamma(4, 1)), whose lowest node at the mode sits on the barrier, made the fallback start at a failed objective (1e40); `nlminb()` then reported relative convergence 0.29 SDs short (shift 0.500 against 0.649). A tighter `rel.tol` (1e-12) or a rerun from the landing point both settle it (2.5e-8 and 2.9e-10 SDs); more iterations do not. The owner chose the cheap fix: rerun once from where it landed, only with a gradient and only when the landing has not settled (about 40 calls here, nothing for fits that settle), as the mode search reruns `nlminb()`. It is documented in the diagnostics vignette and shown by `verbose = TRUE`, since it is a computational step, not a check. **Step cut (October 2026, the owner's).** INLAvaan hands the VB solve to `nlminb()` at the first fixed-point step over 1 SD. On a skewed posterior the first step overshoots and the iteration comes back, so that handover is often needless and, for large m, costly: on the ECPE DINA model (m = 63, with a gradient) the first step was 1.05 SDs, correct against Monte Carlo, and the handover took 54,000 calls (144 s) to reach the point six fixed-point steps reach (to 4e-5 SDs). Now a step over `vb_maxstep` = 1.5 SDs is cut to 1.5 SDs and the iteration goes on; two cut steps in a row, a non-finite step or `vb_maxit` still hand over. The level is a heuristic, like INLAvaan's 1 SD: the fixed point does not depend on it, and 1.5 keeps a step well inside the rule's nodes (up to 3 SDs). The Anderson secant uses the uncut steps and is skipped on a cut one. Tests that relied on a large first step to reach the fallback now force it with `vb_maxit = 1`. `print()`'s count of flagged checks equals the "!" lines of `summary()`.
  - **`diagnostics(object, type = c("global", "param"))`**, an S3 generic. INLAvaan's names, order and print formats, followed by inlavanilla's own (`vb_fallback`, `vb_step_max`, `mean_gap_max`, `ridge_bend_max`, `n_heavy_tails`; `vb_step_sigma`, `mean_gap`, `gamma1`, `tails`, `ridge_bend`). Left out: `nsamp` (draws are on demand), `grad_num`/`grad_diff` (extra calls; a user `grad` is checked at `init` and by its Jacobian asymmetry instead, with separate warnings), `vb_mcse_*` (the cubature rule is deterministic).
  - **`plot(type = c("marg_pdf", "sn_fit", "sn_fit_log"))`**: INLAvaan's `visual_debug()` in base graphics, from the raw scan log-heights and each fit's `logC`, kept in `fit$scan` (no extra calls; `NULL` for Gaussian marginals).
  - **Levels and wording.** The mode-shift, NMAD, unscanned-mass, VB-shift and condition-number levels are INLAvaan's; the VB settling check (0.1 SDs, the mode check's level), tails, ridge bend and the mean gap are inlavanilla's own, and the docs say which is which, with INLAvaan's caveat that every level is a package default, not a calibrated cut-off. The ridge bend level is set from an accuracy target, a marginal SD 10% too small: 0.253 by the normal-mean formula 1 − √(1 − 3b/4) (`lab/bend-threshold.md`). It was 0.1 (a 3.8% target) until October 2026, when the cross-model check in `lab/bend-calibration.R` (51 parameters against grids and HMC) showed that 0.1 flagged errors of 3–5%, among them the intercept of the main vignette, no different from unflagged ones, while 0.25 flags every scan-path narrowing of 10% or more. A posterior SD in the diagnostics is the Laplace SD at the mode, as in INLAvaan. INLAvaan's "healthy range" of 1e-3 to 1e-2 for its `scan_end_mass` is not restored: INLAvaan's docs do state it (with a source in a code comment), but its lower bound fails for healthy fits (an exact Gaussian gives 6.3e-5, the vignette's Poisson 3.8e-4, INLAvaan's own test about 2.5e-4), and the two PolDem marginals that are badly off (0.0031, 0.0019) sit inside it.
  - **Citations.** Jamil and Rue (2026a) use the VB shift (Section 3.1) and NMAD (Section 4.4) as fit diagnostics, the mean gap as an internal check (Section 5.1), and the linear CMP as a first-order approximation to a curved ridge (Section 3.2.1), the basis of `ridge_bend`; Section 4.4 also describes the boundary pile-up a skew-normal cannot capture. The mode shift, condition number and unscanned mass are INLAvaan's `diagnostics()`, not the paper's. INLAvaan's 1,046-fit Spearman calibration is not quoted; the docs cite the paper's Supplementary Material calibration instead.
  - **Names (October 2026).** The owner renamed three diagnostics so that the printed names say what they measure: `bend` became `ridge_bend` ("Ridge bend"), `sn_vs_vb` became `mean_gap` ("Mean gap", after Proposition 3.4), and INLAvaan's `scan_end_mass` became `unscanned_mass` ("Unscanned"), with `_max` versions in the global diagnostics. INLAvaan is to follow, so that the names match again.
- **The copula draws keep the exact quantile map (November 2026).** Adjusting each normal score directly by the marginal's skewness, with no skew-normal quantile, was tried and rejected. The best closed form was the quadratic c₁z + c₂(z² − 1) with the marginal's mean, SD and skewness, its slope held between the exact tail slopes ω and ω/√(1 + α²). It leaves the Gaussian copula unchanged, but its quantiles from 0.5% to 99.5% are up to 0.06 posterior SDs off for |α| ≤ 5, 0.12 at |α| = 10 and 0.18 at 20, against 1e-6 for the spline, and the SD of `exp()` of a parameter with ω = 2 and α ≥ 3 is about 20% too large. All it saves is about 0.6 ms per parameter at 1000 draws, on a step already negligible beside the fit. On four closed-form posteriors the change was small beside the skew-normal's own error, closer to the truth in two and further in two. A moment-matched cubic and a two-slope hyperbola were more accurate in part of the range, but each needs a solve per parameter, and the hyperbola costs half of the exact map. The numbers and a figure are in `lab/ideas/2026-11-copula-skew/`.

- **Posterior draws use `sampling()` only**, matching INLAvaan's exported generic. There is no `draws()` alias, and no `sample()` method, since `base::sample` is not generic.
- **One method per stage**, keeping INLAvaan's defaults:
  - skew-normal marginals
  - `nlminb`
  - the `"shortcut"` volume correction (its gradient route is now batched, see "Batched γ₁" below)
  - for the VB expectation, a degree-5 cubature rule built on a regular simplex (`vb_nodes()`: at most (m + 1)(m + 2) + 1 nodes on the principal axes of Ω, all within 3 SDs), replacing INLAvaan's Sobol points. With a gradient the expected gradient is the weighted mean of the gradient over the nodes, exact when the log-posterior is a degree-6 polynomial in whitened coordinates. Without one, Stein's identity, E[z f(μ + z)] = Ω E[∇f], gives it from `logpost` values at the same nodes, exact up to degree 4. Deterministic either way.
    - History: v0.1 used the fully symmetric degree-5 rule (2m² + 1 nodes), and without a gradient it applied that rule to forward-difference gradients, at (m + 1)(2m² + 1) calls per sweep. In `lab/vb-benchmark.R` that rule was the most accurate on every model tested, from 1 to 30 parameters: 10 to 150 times better than 64 Sobol points in 1–2D, and 0.016 against 0.058 SDs at m = 30.
    - October 2026. The owner set a budget: the VB stage should take at most 1.5–2× the mode-and-Hessian stage, with or without a gradient. The old rule was far over it, so it was replaced (`lab/vb-simplex-benchmark.R`, 11 models from m = 1 to 80):
      - Without a gradient the VB stage went from 1.0–3.8× that stage at m ≤ 3, 8–23× at m = 5–10 and 110–330× at m = 20–80, to 0.65–1.2× up to m = 10, 2.2× at m = 40 and 80, and 3–3.7× on the two near-separated logistic regressions, where the iteration falls back to nlminb(). The 39-parameter SEM of the Rcpp article went from 73× to 1.25×.
      - The error against exact VB went from 1e-4–0.035 SDs to 0.004–0.073 SDs (median 0.015). VB's own error against the posterior mean is 0.01–0.2 SDs on these models, and on balance the new VB means are as close to the posterior means as the old ones: closer on the larger logistic regressions, further on the near-separated ones. A log-gamma posterior with shape 0.5 shows the cost of exactness to degree 4 only: 0.10 SDs from exact VB without a gradient, against 0.03 with one.
      - With a gradient the simplex rule halves the calls at large m (2.0–5.1× to 1.8–3.4× at m = 5–10, 41–100× to 22–52× at m = 20–80) at the same accuracy (errors up to 0.038 against 0.035 SDs; in 2D Radon's seven-point rule is somewhat less accurate than the nine-point rule it replaces).
    - With a gradient the budget is still not met beyond about m = 5 (12× on the SEM), and the owner accepted that. Rules with O(m) nodes would meet it, such as 2m + 2 antithetic points with their covariance matched exactly (exact for degree-4 log-densities, positive weights, more accurate than the old rule at m = 80). They were rejected. Switching rule at some m makes results jump when a parameter is added. Any O(m) rule with positive weights puts its nodes about √(m + 1) SDs out, and in tests fits then aborted near support boundaries that the degree-5 rules survive. INLAvaan's 64 Sobol points were less accurate (0.10 SDs on the SEM) and still over budget at small m.
- **Not ported:**
  - the `asymgaus`, `marggaus` and `sampling` marginals
  - NORTA
  - the `ucminf` and `optim` optimisers
  - Gauss–Hermite VB
  - PPP and DIC

- **An optional `hess`** (owner's decision, October 2026, after first parking it as a v1.1 idea). The package is meant to be reached for instead of `optim()` or `nlminb()` for a MAP, and `nlminb()` takes a Hessian, so `inla_vanilla(logpost, init, grad = NULL, hess = NULL, ...)` does too, after `grad` as in `nlminb()`. It needs `grad` (an error otherwise). Each stage uses it where it can, and falls back to the current route without it:
  - `nlminb()` gets it as `hessian`, in the first run and the restart. Newton steps take far fewer iterations (10 against 87 on PolDem) and land much closer to the mode (whitened gradient 6e-7 against 1e-4), but each costs a Hessian: on PolDem the mode stage is a little slower (29 to 37 ms), on a sparse model faster (16 to 2 ms). On a correlated Gaussian with SDs from 1e-6 to 1e4, started one unit off, it lands on the mode without the restart that the gradient route needs.
  - The Hessian at the mode is the user's, symmetrised, and the curvature scales come from its diagonal (no `fd_scales()`). `hess_discrepancy()` checks it against central differences of `grad` along two fixed whitened directions (all ones, alternating signs), 4 gradient calls whatever m, with a warning above 1e-3.
  - The volume correction gets a third route, `gamma1_hess()`: one Hessian at ϑ* + 0.01 vⱼ gives tr(HΣ) and vⱼᵀHvⱼ, against 2m + 2 gradient calls. It agreed with the `gamma1_grad()` of the time to 1e-9, with the same O(d_outer) error against the exact slope. Removed in November 2026 (see "Batched γ₁").
  - VB, the scans, `scan_bend()` and `loo()` do not use it: VB averages gradients, and `loo()` needs Hessians of `loglik`.
  - Measured with RTMB on five models, warm, median of three fits each, gradient alone against gradient and a Hessian tape (`MakeTape(f, x)$jacfun()$jacfun()`, a little faster than `obj$he`): logistic regression with m = 10, 0.19 to 0.16 s (14%); with m = 40, 8.4 to 6.7 s (20%); the PolDem SEM, m = 39, 0.99 to 0.86 s (13%); a Poisson regression with 50 group intercepts and a slope (an arrow-shaped Hessian), m = 51, 0.64 to 0.42 s (35%); Michaelis–Menten, m = 3, 5 ms either way. The marginals stage was 1.4 to 3.9 times as fast. A dense AD Hessian costs about m gradients (43 at m = 39), a sparse one far fewer (7 at m = 53), and VB, which takes no Hessian, caps the gain. The fits agreed with the gradient route to 2e-5 SDs at most, a difference that comes from the more precise mode. These timings predate "Batched γ₁", after which the default marginals no longer use the Hessian.
  - On a degenerate model (non-centred random intercepts as parameters, joint mode of log τ far from the truth, four diagnostics warnings with or without `hess`), the two routes' marginals differed by SDs. The gradient route stopped 3e-4 SDs from the mode, under the 1e-3 restart level, and the posterior amplified that. Newton steps from there land on the Hessian route's mode.
  - TMB gives no Hessian for a model with random effects (`obj$he()` stops, and `sdreport()` itself takes `optimHess()` of the gradient).
- **Batched γ₁ (November 2026, the owner's).** With a gradient, `gamma1_grad()` takes every parameter at once. With T the third derivative of the log-posterior at the mode, γ₁ⱼ = ½(Σₖ T(Aₖ, Aₖ, vⱼ) − T(vⱼ, vⱼ, vⱼ)), and T(a, a, ·) is the second difference of the gradient along a, so one vector holds the trace part of every parameter: 4m + 1 gradient calls for all m parameters, against 2m² + 2m. This departs from INLAvaan's shortcut.
  - **Why now.** In October 2026 the saving was a few hundred milliseconds on cheap models (m = 40, about 1350 to 910 ms, with the routes agreeing to 6e-4), and the owner judged it not worth doing. On a spatial factor model with 82 parameters, whose 3,078 latent values RTMB integrates out (a gradient costs 48 ms), the whole fit went from 757 s to 193 s, the marginals stage from 651 s to 87 s, and the summaries moved by at most 0.0015 posterior SDs.
  - **The step is 0.1 posterior SDs.** Noise in the gradient enters as 1/h², truncation as h². On that model, against a central-difference reference, the largest error was 5e-3, 6e-4 and 6e-5 at h = 0.01, 0.03 and 0.1, and the old route's was 1.1e-3. On a Poisson regression it is 3.0e-5 at h = 0.1, quarters when h halves (ratio 4.000), and Richardson extrapolation leaves 8e-10.
  - **Principal axes, not the columns of L.** The sum over k is the same for any square root of Σ in the limit, but its truncation is not: with Cholesky columns the fit depends on the order of the parameters by about 1e-5. `principal_axes()`, as in `axis_nodes()`, removes that.
  - **The routes now agree to 2.5e-4, not 2e-7.** `gamma1_lp()` keeps its forward difference 0.01 SDs out, whose error (2.4e-4) `gamma1_grad()` no longer shares.
  - **`hess` no longer touches the default marginals, and `gamma1_hess()` is gone (the owner's).** One Hessian per parameter became the slower route: a Hessian tape costs 41 gradients on the PolDem SEM (m = 39), 7 on an arrow-shaped model, and the break-even is about 4. So 39 Hessians took 140 ms against 14 ms for 4m + 1 gradients, and that fit took 0.30 s with `hess` against 0.16 s without. The Hessian route was also the less accurate, with a forward difference. `hess` now gives the Newton steps, the covariance at the mode, and the scans of `"cmp"` and `"tk"`, where a conditional Hessian is needed at each point (one Hessian against 2(m - 1) gradients). Fits with and without it agree to 5e-7 SDs in the summaries, and the PolDem fit takes 0.18 s against 0.17 s, for a mode found about a hundred times more precisely. Turning on `width = "cmp"` when `hess` is supplied was rejected: 780 Hessians, about 2.8 s, and supplying a derivative should change how a quantity is computed, not which quantity.
- **The ridge bend without a gradient (November 2026, the owner's concern about its cost).** The check was meant to be cheap, two gradient calls per parameter. Without a gradient it took a one-sided difference in each other parameter at ±2 SDs, 2(m − 1) calls per parameter: 43% of the marginals stage's calls at m = 40, as many as the volume correction. Now `gamma1_lp()` gives the direction for free. In whitened coordinates the gradient along the scan is g(t) = g(0) − t a + ½t² T(a, a, ·) + …, with a = L[j, ]/sⱼ, so g(0.01) − g(0) + 0.01 a, without its part along a, points across the ridge, and those gradients are the odd part of the differences `gamma1_lp()` already takes for the trace (`whitened_lp()`). `bend_along()` takes the derivative along that direction at ±2 SDs, one call per point. It is a lower bound on the full gain, and the full differences are taken only above `bend_screen` = 0.1. So every value that can reach the flag is the full one, and the calibration of the 0.25 level carries over. With a gradient nothing changes, and `width = "cmp"` and `"tk"` keep the full differences, which are negligible beside those scans.
  - **Evidence** (`lab/bend-screen.R`: 351 parameters of 28 models, m = 2 to 42, among them the calibration models of `lab/bend-calibration.R`, the PolDem SEM, and regressions with several noise scales and random intercepts). The flag agrees on 350 of 351, with no false flags. The one miss is the intercept of a Poisson random-intercept model whose joint mode has collapsed (log τ = −29.5), where the other nine parameters are flagged and the second-order term vanishes. Where the full value is above 0.02, the median ratio to it is 1.000 and the 5th percentile 0.965. The marginals do not change, since the volume correction comes from the same calls in the same way.
  - **The screen is needed.** The direction alone missed three flags, among them the only flagged parameter of the SEM (`logv_dem65`, 0.23 against the full 0.44, where the bend is not leading-order). Screens of 0.05, 0.1 and 0.125 all leave the one miss, and refine 191, 137 and 128 parameters. A bend at the flag level escapes a screen of 0.1 only if the direction holds under 40% of its gain. 0.1 was the flag level until October 2026, a marginal SD 4% too small in the reference family.
  - **The gain.** Calls of `logpost` in a whole fit fell by 14–23% for healthy models at m = 20 to 41, 16% on the SEM, and by 0–4% where nearly every ridge bends (random intercepts), which pay what they did. Whole fits without a gradient at m ≥ 20 were 1.13–1.31× as fast, and the marginals stage 1.26–1.69×.
- **Parallel scans through future (November 2026, the owner's).** Not ported from INLAvaan, which batches: it runs `cores` parameters, waits for all of them, then starts the next set. The per-parameter loop of `fit_marginals()` and the calls of `loglik` in `taylor_pointwise()` run in parallel through `future`, which is in Suggests, with the plain loop as the full fallback. The switch is `cores` (the owner's, after first trying `future::plan()` alone): `cores = 8` sets up workers for that fit and restores the user's plan afterwards, `cores = NULL`, the default, follows `future::plan()`, and `cores = 1` is serial whatever the plan. `loo()` and `waic()` follow the fit's setting, as they follow `fit$silent`. `plan()` alone made the user choose `multicore` or `multisession`, and choosing `multisession` sent compiled models back to serial. `cores` chooses: forks where `par_can_fork()` says they are safe, and separate sessions elsewhere. `future::supportsMulticore()` knows about Windows and RStudio but returns `TRUE` inside Positron, whose ark kernel refuses to fork ("Can't fork the R session in Positron", ark 0.1.252, tested in a private kernel), so `par_can_fork()` checks for the `ark` program. Not the `POSITRON` variable, which can reach integrated terminals, where plain R forks well. A wrong guess fails safely, since ark's error ends in the serial fallback. A package should not start workers unasked, so with neither `cores` nor a plan the fit is serial, and an interactive fit expected to scan for over 10 s says so once (`par_hint()`). Whatever the switch, the answer is the serial one, in summaries, diagnostics, counts and warnings.
  - **Shape.** One job per worker at most, parameters dealt out in turn (`par_groups()`), all started at once, as `future_lapply()` does by default. In simulation with four workers that was within 4% of handing out parameters one at a time. A job signals an immediate condition after each item (`par_tick()`), relayed live while the parent polls `resolved()`, so the existing cli line moves one item at a time, with no `progressr`. Direct `future()` calls rather than `future_lapply()`: one dependency, and its scan for globals picks up the `...` bound in `logpost_x` and passes it to the job.
  - **Safety.** Separate sessions cannot run compiled functions, and they do not always say so: Rcpp and TMB stop with an error, but RTMB with random effects returned `NaN` with no error, which gave marginals up to 2.19 SDs off in a prototype. So `par_setup()` runs `probe` on a worker and in the parent, with the real functions and data, and any difference or error means serial with a message. `multicore` shares everything and passes. In Positron's console compiled code has to be rebuilt on each worker, which a `logpost` can do itself by comparing `Sys.getpid()` with the parent's (the recipe in `?inla_vanilla`, tested in a private ark on the articles' code): `MakeADFun()` inside a function, or `Rcpp::sourceCpp()` with a shared `cacheDir`. RTMB without random effects needs none of this. `obj$retape()` was rejected for the recipe: it reruns a function whose global data `future` cannot see through `obj`. A job that fails for any reason, an error or a killed worker, is done again in the parent, so an error reads as in a serial fit (`scan_parameter()` is the same code both ways).
  - **How many jobs.** The time with w jobs is about est_s / w + cost w, least at w = sqrt(est_s / cost). `cost` is the probe's elapsed time, the real cost of reaching a worker with the real data. `est_s` is the time per call so far times `scan_calls()`, or for `loo()` the shortest of the first three calls times the calls (the byte compiler slows the first ones of a session). A scan under `par_min_s` = 1 s stays serial without touching `future`: the gain below that was a few hundred milliseconds at best. Timing a first parameter instead was rejected: on 12 workers one serial parameter of a `"tk"` fit is 50% of the time left.
  - **Cost of a future.** `serialize()` was 79% of the parent's time in a prototype that shipped its whole frame. `logpost`, `grad` and `hess` share an environment, and future serialises each global separately, so they travel as one list, which sends the data once. On a 6.4 MB model that took 12 workers from 1.05× to 1.55× (7 jobs chosen).
  - **Evidence** (macOS, M4 Pro with 10 performance and 4 efficiency cores). Long `"tk"` fits scaled to about 10 workers: logistic, m = 40, 25.9 s, 3.5 s at 10 and 3.6 s at 12 (`multicore`), 3.5 s at 12 (`multisession`); RTMB random effects, m = 21, 32.0 s, 4.5 s at 12. An earlier benchmark that peaked at five processes (a pairwise likelihood) does not carry over. With the real code on 12 workers the fits were 7.3–7.9× faster, and identical to serial in summaries, counts and warnings. Default fits stop improving at about 6 workers, 1.2–1.5×, because the loop is about half the fit. All R processes together peaked at 3.3 GB. Not run on Windows or inside RStudio or Positron.
  - **Rcpp does not help.** R's manual strongly discourages calling its API from threads, so threads cannot run `logpost`, and C++ calling an R closure costs the same. Only `fit_skew_normal()` could move, about 1 ms per parameter: 73% of a 55 ms fit, 3% or less of fits over a second. A `src/` directory would also need a compiler for every GitHub install.
  - **Open.** Only the loop runs in parallel, so default fits are capped at 1.6–2.3× however many cores. The Hessian differences, the VB nodes, the gain ratio and the shared γ₁ are independent evaluations too and could use the same helper, for a best case of 2.7–8.6× at 12 workers. They would pay only when `logpost` is slow, since each batch costs a trip to the workers. To decide with real timings.
- **The gradient is compared at the mode, not at `init` (November 2026, the owner's: "wrong grads are the user's problem", get into the fit straight away, checks along the way or at the end, speed is key).** The old check ran before the first progress line: `fd_scales()` at `init` and two sets of central differences, about 8m to 12m `logpost` calls. On an RTMB model with m = 21 and 1000 random effects it was 175 calls and 1.9 s of a 13.8 s fit, all of it on a blank screen. It gave the fit nothing: without it a correct gradient gave identical results on a plain R model, and agreed to within 1e-6 on RTMB, where the warm starts differ.
  - **What replaced it.** At `init`, one call of each function, for shape and finiteness. At the mode, `grad_gap()` reads what `fd_scales()` has computed for the steps of the Hessian anyway: the central and second differences of `logpost` along each coordinate, against `gr(mode)` and the diagonal of the Hessian taken from the gradient. No calls. The gap is the larger of the difference in slope per conditional SD (about how far it moves the mode) and half the relative difference in curvature (about the relative error in an SD), flagged above 0.1: the level of the mode check, and the 10% of the ridge bend. Half, because a comparison of the curvature itself read 0.097 on a correct gradient (Student-t, 5 df, scale 1e-6). With `hess` there is no `fd_scales()` to read, so `grad_gap` is `NA`. The VB `budget` is now what its comment says, the calls of the mode and Hessian stage: it used to include the check's.
  - **What it catches.** On logistic and normal models: twice too large 0.50, one element 1.5 times too large 0.25, by name, the log-Jacobian forgotten at n = 30 0.12, an informative prior forgotten 1.03. Not flagged, and harmless: a weak prior forgotten (0.004), 10% too large (0.05). In all of these the marginals barely moved (means by at most 0.04 SDs, SDs by at most 3%), because the scans use `logpost` itself: a wrong gradient mostly harms the log marginal likelihood and the covariance. A gradient of the wrong sign stops the fit at the mode, and the Hessian error now says so. A skew-symmetric error has the right diagonal and is zero at the mode: the symmetry of the Jacobian takes those, as before.
  - **False alarms.** On correct gradients, across a Gaussian with SDs from 1e-6 to 1e4, beta-binomials down to 0 of 10, a gamma-Poisson, a normal with n = 8, a logistic with m = 40 and RTMB with random effects, the gap was at most 0.026. Student-t posteriors at scales of 1e-6 read up to 0.079, and that is the worst case that can arise in practice: the step of `fd_scales()` reaches a whole conditional SD only when the SD is below about 1e-4 of the parameter's size. A posterior that tight and that heavy-tailed (3 df) can read up to 0.15, which was accepted.
  - **The progress line.** Every 16th call ticked it, so a slow `logpost` drew nothing for 16 calls. A first call of 10 ms or more now makes every call tick (`prog$every`; an update that draws nothing costs 1.4 µs), and the 0.5 s delay of the first line counts from the start of the fit.
- **One-step VB is the default (November 2026, the owner's).** The budget was tightened: the VB stage should cost no more than the mode and Hessian stage, and be as accurate as possible within it. It measured 1.2–21× that stage with a gradient. `vb` is now `"onestep"` (the default), `"full"` or `"off"`, with `FALSE` still meaning `"off"` and `TRUE` the default. `"onestep"` is the first step of the fixed-point iteration, and stops. With a gradient it averages over 2m + 1 nodes (`axis_nodes()`: the centre and ±√3 SDs along each principal axis), 0.10–0.36× the budget. Without one it is one Stein pass on `vb_nodes()`, 0.13–0.58×, since Stein's identity needs that rule's degree. The fixed-point map has slope O(1/n), so the first step holds most of the movement, and exact VB is O(n^-3/2) SDs from the posterior mean anyway, so iterating exchanges one small remainder for another. On 133 simulated datasets it was closer to the posterior mean than the converged solver on 82, tied on 48 and worse on 3 (`lab/ideas/2026-11-one-step-vb`). A first step over 2.5 SDs (`vb_onestep_guard`), or a failed node, goes on to iterate with capped steps, within the calls of the mode and Hessian stage, and never reaches `nlminb()` on account of its length. The level is a heuristic. `vb_step_sigma` is `NA` after a single step, and a check that did not run is not a flag, so the fit counts as converged on the mode check alone. The method departs from INLAvaan, which the owner has set aside: inlavanilla is now the reference.
- **The gain ratio checks the one step (November 2026, the owner's concern that it "works for some models but not others").** One step assumes that the expected log-posterior is a parabola with the Laplace curvature, which promises a rise of ½δᵀHδ. `vb_gain_ratio` is the rise delivered, on `axis_nodes()` (4m + 2 calls of `logpost`, with or without a gradient), over that promise, and it is flagged below 0.5 (`warn_levels$vb_gain`). It warns and does not act: switching to `"full"` on a low ratio helped on 5 flagged fits and hurt on 15, all random-intercept models where no Gaussian-centred method is close. A curvature check along the step, which costs nothing, caught only the four most extreme failures. The ratio cannot see a long tail far from the mode, as on the SEM's log variances, where the mean gap has to. It is left out of "converged", as the VB shift is, because it judges the approximation and not the computation. See `lab/ideas/2026-11-one-step-vb`.

## Findings to discuss

- **Linear scan path at small n.** The skew-normal scan follows the conditional mode path (CMP) of the Laplace Gaussian, which is a straight line (Lemma 3.1). When another parameter's conditional mode bends along that path, the scanned heights are too low in the tails. A common case is a noise scale, where σ̂²(K) ∝ SSR(K) bends quadratically. The marginal then comes out too narrow, by a factor that shrinks as O(1/n).
  - Example: Michaelis–Menten on the treated `Puromycin` data (n = 12; parameters log Vm, log K, log σ).
  - Exact marginal SDs from a 3D grid: 0.0382 and 0.1537 for log Vm and log K.
  - Skew-normal: 0.0315 and 0.127, both about 18% too narrow. Laplace is the same.
  - A full Laplace marginal with exact conditional modes and conditional Hessians recovers 0.0383 and 0.1541. So the gap comes from the linear path, not from the skew-normal fit or the volume correction.
  - log σ itself is captured almost exactly: skew-normal mean 2.4733 against an exact 2.4734, while Laplace gives 2.3324.
  - The test suite found the same effect independently:
    - For the normal mean under a normal–inverse-gamma prior, the skew-normal SD is short by exactly √(2(aₙ − 1)/(2aₙ + 1)), a Gaussian against the true Student-t.
    - For the log scale of a Weibull model (n ≈ 15), both the skew-normal and Laplace SDs are about 11% too small, and a full Laplace marginal is within 0.1%.
  - Prototype remedies on Puromycin (`lab/scan-path-prototype.R`), SD error for log Vm and log K:

    | Scan | SD error |
    |---|---|
    | Current | −17.7% |
    | One Newton step towards the conditional mode (Laplace curvature) | −8.1% |
    | Three Newton steps | −5.7% |
    | Full Laplace: exact conditional mode and conditional log-determinant at each scan point | −1.0% |

    So the bent path accounts for about two thirds of the gap, and the linear volume correction for the rest.
  - **Decision.** Keep the CMP and the shortcut volume correction, which make the method fast. Document the limit in the Accuracy section of `?inla_vanilla`, and flag it with the cheap `ridge_bend` diagnostic (named `bend` until October 2026): two gradient calls per parameter, flagged above 0.25 nats (0.1 at first; see "Levels and wording").
  - The full-Laplace remedy is INLA's "Laplace strategy" for marginals. It needs a conditional optimisation and a conditional Hessian at each of the 21 scan points: about 21(m + 3) gradient calls per parameter, against 2m + 2 now, so roughly 10× the cost of the marginal stage. It is a change of method and needs a decision.
  - In this example the mean gap (then `sn_vs_vb`) flagged log σ (0.13 SDs). That gap came from the VB mean being off, not the skew-normal, so the diagnostic is documented as "either may be off".

- **LOO/WAIC (added after v0.1.0, when the owner made it non-negotiable for model comparison).** INLAvaan's deterministic second-order Taylor LOO and WAIC (Alhyari, Jamil, Montcho and Rue, 2026, "Deterministic leave-one-cluster-out cross-validation for multilevel Bayesian models", arXiv:2609.00670 [stat.ME]; `R/loo.R` and `R/method-loo.R` in INLAvaan): per-unit score s_u and Hessian H_u of the pointwise log-likelihood at the VB mean, with the Laplace covariance Ω; log CPO_u = ℓ_u − ½ s_uᵀ(Ω⁻¹ + H_u)⁻¹ s_u + ½ log|I + ΩH_u|, lpd_u likewise with −H_u, p_waic,u = s_uᵀΩs_u + ½ tr[(H_uΩ)²].   - Results are shaped like the loo package's objects, so `loo::loo_compare()` works with loo only in Suggests. The methods are registered on loo's generics too, and the default methods forward other objects to loo, so attaching inlavanilla after loo, rstan or brms does not break them.

  - `loo()` and `waic()` inherit `silent` from the fit (owner's decision), including the warnings of `loglik`, which are recorded in the result.
  - Checked in the vignette against 54 brute-force refits (warpbreaks, negative binomial). The sampling route still works: `loo::loo(sampling(fit, 4000, fun = loglik, ...))`.

If INLAvaan later uses inlavanilla as its engine, the generics should live here. `diagnostics.default()` passes an S4 object on to INLAvaan's `diagnostics()` when the INLAvaan namespace is loaded, as `loo.default()` does for loo; the other way round needs INLAvaan's S4 generic to gain a `useAsDefault`.
- **Transform layer, as a wrapper around the engine.** Given `transform`, build an unconstrained `logpost(ginv(ϑ)) + log|det J|` and its gradient, run the unchanged v1 engine, then post-process the summaries. Size-1 blocks map the skew-normal back analytically; joint blocks go through the draws. It would support:
  - named elementwise transforms (`"log"`, `"logit"`, `"tanh"`);
